### NOTES
The central difficulty is that the price of a pair is a maximum of three additive quantities, so the objective is not additive over pairs or over individual cakes. A key reduction is to assign each pair to one of the three attributes that attains the maximum for that pair, and to label both cakes in the pair with that attribute. Then the total price is exactly the sum of the labeled attribute values, and each attribute label is used an even number of times because it appears twice in each pair of that type. Conversely, any selection of 2K cakes with an even number of cakes labeled by each attribute can be paired within labels, and the resulting pair prices are at least the labeled sum. Therefore the original pairing problem is equivalent to choosing 2K cakes and labeling each with one of three attributes, with even label counts, maximizing the sum of chosen attribute values.

Let M_i be the maximum of the three values of cake i. Any feasible labeled selection is bounded by the sum of M_i over its selected cakes, hence by the sum of the largest 2K values of M_i. Taking the top 2K cakes by M_i and labeling each cake by one of its maximizing attributes gives a baseline that attains this upper bound except possibly for the parity constraint. If the three label parities are all even, this baseline is optimal. Otherwise, because 2K is even, the parity mask has exactly two 1-bits.

Since the baseline uses the top 2K cakes by M_i, any recoloring of a selected cake or replacement of a selected cake by an unselected one has nonnegative loss. Any feasible final solution can be decomposed into recolor operations on cakes that remain selected and swap operations replacing a selected cake by an unselected cake. Each such operation flips the parity of exactly two labels. With only three labels, fixing a two-bit parity mask can be done by either one operation with that mask or two operations whose masks xor to it; duplicate operations can be canceled without increasing loss. Thus an optimal correction should be representable by at most two non-conflicting local operations. The main risk is not the high-level reduction but computing the cheapest such operations safely: ties in M_i at the 2K boundary, ties among maximizing attributes, and the possibility that two individually cheap operations use the same cake.

### NEXT
- Baseline top-2K-by-M plus parity correction: sort cakes by M_i, take the first 2K as the baseline, compute the baseline sum and parity mask. For each directed label change, collect a small constant number of best recolor operations on selected cakes and best swap operations formed from low-M selected cakes of the source label and high-value unselected cakes of the target label, keeping cake identities. Evaluate the best single operation for the odd mask and the best pair of operations involving the third label, rejecting pairs that share a cake. Pitfalls: arbitrary tie-breaking at the 2K boundary may require zero-cost swaps among tied cakes; tied maximum attributes require zero-cost recolors; one best operation per directed change is not enough because two swaps may need distinct unselected cakes or two operations from the same source label may need distinct selected cakes; when K=N/2 swaps are unavailable; use 64-bit integers.
- Min-cost-flow residual-cycle formulation: model selection of 2K item-label assignments as a flow ignoring parity, then view parity fixing as sending flow around residual cycles that flip two label parities. Compress the relevant cycles into recolor and swap operations and solve a tiny parity-state shortest path. Pitfalls: standard min-cost flow does not enforce even label counts; naive cycle enumeration is too large; must ensure combined cycles are vertex-disjoint or account for conflicts; this approach may essentially reduce to the same local-exchange computation.
- Lagrangian relaxation with parity DP: penalize each selected cake by a parameter lambda and run an 8-state DP over parity masks, where each cake is either skipped or chosen with one label, tracking counts or tie-breaking to force exactly 2K selected cakes; binary search lambda. Pitfalls: the value as a function of selected count may not be concave under the even-label constraint, or the optimal count may jump over 2K; recovering the exact value for 2K with ties is subtle; the method may be slower and less reliable than the exchange argument.
- Fixed label-count assignment: choose how many pairs use each label, with the three pair-counts summing to K, and solve the resulting 3-way assignment with exact even demands, then optimize over the counts. Pitfalls: enumerating counts is quadratic or worse; the assignment with three labels and item conflicts is nontrivial and may require min-cost flow or Lagrange multipliers with tie handling; convexity in the counts is not obvious and exact optimization may be hard.
- Candidate-pool exact DP over exceptions: prove or assume that an optimal solution differs from the top-2K baseline by only a constant number of cakes/labels, build a small pool from top M_i, top values per attribute, and boundary ties, then solve the exact selection/labeling problem on the pool with DP over parity and number of exceptions. Pitfalls: the constant pool size must be justified; boundary ties can be numerous and may require careful handling; the DP must correctly account for which baseline cakes are removed and their baseline contributions; a too-small pool can miss the optimum.
- Direct matching or threshold-based approaches: treat the problem as maximum weight K matching in a complete graph with edge weight equal to the maximum of three sums, or try to binary search a total price and test feasibility. Pitfalls: general maximum matching is far too slow for N up to 1e5; greedy edge selection is not correct; a total-price threshold does not decompose cleanly over pairs; these are useful only as sanity checks, not likely primary algorithms.
