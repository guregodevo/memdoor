### NOTES
The core difficulty is that the subsequence choice couples three things at once: order, selected-length parity, and a multiplicative objective under a cap. The alternating sum depends on whether the next chosen element lands at an even or odd position in the chosen subsequence, so parity must be tracked. The product is not additive, but the limit is small, so product can be discretized. The trickiest parts are the special behavior of zero and overflow: a product that exceeds the limit is not necessarily dead if a later zero resets it to zero; product zero is absorbing for all later positive values; and the empty subsequence must not be counted even though it would naturally have sum zero and a neutral product.

Useful bounds make a DP feasible: the total sum of all numbers is at most 150 * 12 = 1800, so any alternating sum lies in a range of width at most 3601. The product only needs states from 0 through limit, plus one overflow state meaning “greater than limit.” A tempting but unsafe simplification is to keep only the maximum product for each parity and sum; that can fail because a smaller intermediate product may remain under the limit after future multiplications while a larger one overflows, and a zero product may become preferable if all positive products would overflow before a later zero.

The supplied plan is strong: use parity and capped product as explicit DP dimensions, and represent the set of achievable alternating sums for each such state as a bitset. The main implementation risks are avoiding use of the current element more than once, seeding non-empty subsequences correctly, preserving overflow states until a zero can reset them, masking the sum bitset to the valid range, and keeping the per-element update fast enough in Python.

### NEXT
- Product-indexed bitset DP, matching the supplied plan: maintain, for each parity and capped product, a bitset of achievable alternating sums; process each number with skip and take transitions; seed single-element subsequences separately; keep an overflow product state; scan products downward at the end. Pitfalls: in-place updates can let the same element be taken twice; overflow must be retained because a later zero can turn it into valid product zero; zero product must be handled as absorbing; the empty subsequence must not create a false answer for k = 0; bitsets must be masked to the valid sum range; looping over all product states may need active-state pruning or special handling for values 0 and 1 for speed.

- Zero/positive decomposition: split the problem into product-zero feasibility and positive-product optimization. For product zero, run a cheap parity/sum DP with a flag indicating whether a zero has been selected. For positive product, ignore zeros entirely and DP over products 1..limit, since any positive final product cannot include a zero and any prefix product exceeding limit cannot later become a valid positive product. Pitfalls: the zero-feasibility DP must distinguish empty, non-empty without zero, and has-zero states; zeros can change parity without changing sum, so they must be allowed to flip parity; the positive DP must not use zeros as parity-changing elements; the final answer is the best positive product if one exists, otherwise zero if zero-feasible, otherwise -1.

- Sum-indexed DP with product sets or product bitsets: maintain, for each parity and alternating sum, the set of capped products that can achieve it. Updating with a number multiplies each stored product by the number and caps it, with zero collapsing all products to zero. Pitfalls: multiplication is not a simple bitset shift over product indices, so updates are more awkward than sum shifts; Python sets may become memory-heavy; product bitsets require precomputed mappings or per-bit extraction; dominance pruning is unsafe unless carefully justified because smaller products can avoid overflow and zero can reset overflow.

- Pareto-frontier or dominance-pruned DP: for each parity and sum, store only a reduced collection of product states believed to be nondominated, hoping to cut the product dimension. Pitfalls: simple “larger product dominates smaller product” is false under a cap because a larger product may overflow on a future multiplication while a smaller one stays valid; zero further complicates dominance because an overflow state can become zero while a positive state cannot; a safe dominance relation may require knowledge of the remaining suffix, which can make the pruning as hard as the original problem.

- Meet-in-the-middle over two halves: compute reachable parity/sum/product states for a prefix half and a suffix half, then combine them. When concatenating, if the prefix subsequence length is odd, the signs of the suffix alternating sum are flipped; products are multiplied with capping and zero handling. Pitfalls: even with n = 150, half-state sets can be large; combining states can become quadratic unless carefully indexed; the parity-dependent sign flip is easy to get wrong; zero and overflow interactions must be handled in both halves and in the combination; memory for two large state tables may be high.

- Top-down memoized search or branch-and-bound: recursively decide skip/take for each index, memoizing by position, parity, current alternating sum, and current capped product or remaining product capacity, and pruning using bounds on remaining possible sum and product. Pitfalls: the state space can still be enormous because both sum and product dimensions are large; recursion depth is manageable but memoization may blow memory; strong pruning is needed to avoid exponential behavior; exactness is hard to preserve if bounds are too aggressive; product-zero and overflow-reset cases need special treatment.

- Candidate-product feasibility or factor-exponent DP: enumerate candidate final products from limit down to 0 and test whether any subsequence has that exact product and alternating sum k, or represent positive products by prime-exponent vectors for values 2..12 with a numeric cap. Pitfalls: testing products separately repeats work; exact positive product forbids zeros, while product zero is a separate feasibility problem; ones do not change product but affect sum and parity, so they must still be handled; exponent-vector states may be numerous and cap checks are needed; order-dependent subsequence feasibility remains the hard part.
