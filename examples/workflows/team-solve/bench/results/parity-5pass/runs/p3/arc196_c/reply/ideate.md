### NOTES
The easy part is the graph-theoretic reduction. The original path edges only go forward, so a prefix of vertices is “closed” exactly when no added edge goes from a white vertex after the prefix to a black vertex inside the prefix. Since added edges are only white-to-black, it is enough to inspect prefixes ending just before a black vertex. If such a prefix contains k black vertices and A_k white vertices, the cut is automatically safe when A_k < k; otherwise it is safe iff at least one of the first k black vertices is paired with a white vertex of rank greater than A_k, i.e. the prefix maximum of the black-to-white permutation exceeds A_k. Also, if vertex 1 is white it has no incoming edge at all, and if vertex 2N is black it has no outgoing edge at all, so the answer is immediately 0 in those cases.

The hard part is counting permutations p of [N] (black rank -> white rank) satisfying prefix-maximum lower bounds of the form max_{j≤k} p_j > A_k for certain k. The forbidden events E_k = “first k entries are all ≤ A_k” are not nested when A_k changes. Keeping only the smallest k for each distinct value A_k removes redundant events, but the remaining events still have a two-parameter structure: a black-count m and a white-count b. Inclusion-exclusion over these minimal events gives a triangular DP or path-sum over chains of events. Each chain contributes a product of factorial ratios, and the resulting recurrence needs, for each new event, a sum over all previous events of the form x_c * fact[b - m_c].

The core algorithmic difficulty is evaluating this online sum for up to N events. It resembles convolution with the factorial kernel, but the update index is m_c while the query index is b, and the coefficient x_c is only known when the event with white-count b is processed. The gap b - m_c can be large, so a naive divide-and-conquer convolution may have unexpectedly large ranges. Additional pitfalls include duplicate A_k values, the causal restriction that only earlier events may contribute, modular inverses for factorial denominators, the no-event case, and the endpoint zero cases.

### NEXT
- Inclusion-exclusion over minimal prefix events, then evaluate the triangular recurrence with an online convolution structure such as CDQ divide-and-conquer plus NTT or a relaxed convolution. Treat each event as an update at black-count m with coefficient x and a query at white-count b, contributing x * fact[b - m]. Pitfalls: the coefficient is delayed until b is reached; naive CDQ over event order may need very long NTT ranges when b - m is large; must exclude current and future events; duplicate A_k must be collapsed to the smallest k; endpoint and no-event cases must be handled separately.
- Direct DP over the current prefix maximum. Process black vertices one by one, with state equal to the maximum white rank used so far; transitions use the number of unused smaller whites to keep the maximum and prefix sums to jump to a larger maximum. Pitfalls: this is naturally O(N^2) in time; boundary zeroing when the maximum is too small must be applied carefully; optimizing it to near-linear time is nontrivial and may require a non-obvious transform.
- Polynomial or falling-factorial representation of the prefix-maximum DP. Express the DP vector as coefficients of a polynomial in falling factorials; the unconstrained transition becomes bidiagonal, while the boundary condition contributes a single dot product involving the current lower limit. Potentially batch steps or use generating functions. Pitfalls: the polynomial degree grows to N; the boundary dot product may be O(degree) unless maintained cleverly; deriving a correct fast closed form for blocks is subtle and error-prone.
- Inverse-permutation / right-to-left minima reformulation. Assign each white vertex a black position, turning the condition into suffix-minimum constraints: for each relevant white-count b, the minimum position among whites greater than b must be at most m_b. Count by enumerating right-to-left minima or by a DP over the current minimum position. Pitfalls: the constraints are OR-type rather than simple per-position bounds; record enumeration has many cases; the natural DP is O(N^2), and a fast acceleration is not obvious.
- Determinant, rook-polynomial, or nonintersecting-paths reduction. Try to express the count as a determinant of a matrix of binomial or factorial coefficients, or via a combinatorial correspondence such as RSK, then exploit the matrix structure for fast computation. Pitfalls: finding the correct determinant formula is difficult; a generic O(N^3) determinant is too slow; the matrix may be dense and only triangular/Hankel-like, possibly leading back to the same online convolution problem.
- Sqrt/block decomposition for the online sum. Keep a buffer of recent event coefficients and answer queries by naive summation over the buffer; periodically flush the buffer by convolving it with the factorial kernel or by precomputing block contributions. Pitfalls: complexity is roughly O(N sqrt N log N) or worse; flushing with full-length NTT per block can be expensive; choosing a block size suitable for Python is delicate; delayed updates and large index gaps still need careful handling.
- Balance/run-length segmentation. Decompose the event sequence into segments where the balance b - m is constant or slowly varying; within a segment the kernel is a shifted factorial and standard online convolution may apply, with transitions between segments handled by precomputed contributions. Pitfalls: the balance can change at almost every event, making segments length one; worst-case behavior may degenerate to O(N^2) or many small NTTs; a robust fallback is needed.
