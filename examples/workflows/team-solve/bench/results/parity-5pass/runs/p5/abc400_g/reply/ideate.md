### NOTES
The key reduction is to replace each pair by the attribute that attains its maximum. If a pair is assigned to color c, both cakes contribute their c-value, and each color must be used an even number of times. Conversely, any even-color selection can be paired by color and gives at least that sum, while the pair's true maximum gives an equal coloring, so the two formulations are equivalent. Let m_i be the maximum of the three values of cake i. Ignoring parity, the best total is the sum B of the largest 2K values m_i, and this is also a global upper bound for the original pairing problem. The core difficulty is therefore not the pairing itself but the parity constraint on the three colors, especially because the unconstrained optimum may be non-unique due to ties in m_i at the cutoff and ties among the attributes attaining m_i.

The provided plan is the most direct: choose any canonical top-2K baseline, color each selected cake by one maximizing attribute, compute the parity mask. If it is zero, answer B. Otherwise two colors are odd; a recolor of a selected cake or a swap of a selected cake with an unselected cake toggles exactly two color parities. With three colors, any needed parity correction can be reduced to either one direct toggle or two toggles through the third color; extra operations form a parity-zero cycle and can be removed because all losses relative to the baseline are nonnegative. The remaining task is to compute the cheapest one- or two-operation correction.

Pitfalls for this exchange approach: first, ties. A fixed arbitrary baseline may have odd parity even though another top-2K choice or another choice of maximizing attribute has even parity and loss zero. The candidate search must include zero-cost recolors to tied maximizing attributes and zero-cost swaps among cakes with equal cutoff m. Second, candidate ranking. Recolor candidates must be ranked by m_i minus the new attribute value, not by m_i alone. Swap candidates separate into the selected side, needing the smallest m among selected cakes of the old baseline color, and the unselected side, needing the largest value in the new color among unselected cakes. Third, item conflicts. The cheapest operation of type A-C and the cheapest of type B-C may use the same cake; a naive sum of independent minima can be invalid. Top-two candidates per relevant role and explicit cake-id conflict checks, or a small DP, are needed. Fourth, edge cases such as N=2K where no unselected cakes exist, K=1, all zero values, and 64-bit totals.

A second implementation of the same reduction is to collect a small pool of candidate cakes: for each baseline color, top two selected cakes by smallest m for swaps out; for each ordered recolor, top two selected cakes by smallest recolor loss; for each attribute, top two unselected cakes by largest attribute value. Then run a tiny DP over the pool items, tracking parity change and the net change in the number of selected cakes, and require final parity even and net selected change zero. This automatically handles conflicts and ties. Its pitfalls are that the pool must be proven sufficient, and that unselected items can have negative individual loss, so the DP must allow negative intermediate values while the final loss is nonnegative.

A genuinely different approach is Lagrangian relaxation: add a penalty lambda per selected cake and solve, for each lambda, an 8-state parity DP over all cakes to maximize adjusted value with even color counts. Binary search lambda to force about 2K selections and recover the exact answer. Pitfalls: the value as a function of selected count may not be concave once parity and per-cake color choices are present; tie-breaking counts may not give exactly 2K; recovering the exact optimum from the Lagrangian upper bound is subtle. It is fast to prototype but risky as the final solution.

Another conceptual formulation is a min-cost flow or fixed-count assignment: choose even counts for the three colors and assign cakes to colors. For fixed counts this is a 3-color b-matching, but enumerating counts is too large, and a generic flow with 2K units is too slow. Parity constraints are not ordinary capacities and would need parity-state nodes. This is useful mainly for small-N validation or theoretical understanding.

Finally, a brute-force exact solver for very small N, such as DP over cakes, selected count, and parity mask, or exhaustive pairing for tiny N, is valuable for random testing of the main approaches, but it is not scalable to 1e5.

### NEXT
- Implement the baseline top-2K plus parity-correction exchange algorithm: compute baseline parity, then minimum loss for one direct operation or two operations through the third color, using top-two candidates per directed recolor, per selected-color swap-out, and per unselected-attribute swap-in, with explicit cake-id conflict checks.
- Implement the same reduction as a small candidate-pool DP over parity mask and selection balance as a cross-check or alternative primary implementation, ensuring the pool includes top-two candidates for all relevant roles and handles negative intermediate losses.
- Prototype a Lagrangian/Aliens-trick solver with an 8-state parity DP and binary search on a per-cake penalty, and validate it against brute force on small random cases to see whether exact cardinality recovery is reliable.
- Develop a small-N brute-force checker, using DP over cakes, selected count, and parity mask or exhaustive pairing for tiny N, to generate random tests and verify the main solution, especially tie-heavy cases.
- Optionally explore a min-cost flow or fixed-color-count assignment formulation for small N or as a theoretical sanity check, but avoid it for the final large-N solution unless a specialized greedy/flow optimization is found.
