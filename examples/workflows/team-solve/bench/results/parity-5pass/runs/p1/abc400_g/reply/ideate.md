### NOTES
The core difficulty is that the objective is a maximum-weight K-matching on a complete graph with edge weight defined as the maximum of three additive attribute sums. A direct matching approach is impossible for N up to 10^5. The key structural simplification is that each pair can be charged to one of the three attributes that attains its maximum. Then each attribute is charged by an even number of cakes, because every pair contributes two cakes to the attribute chosen for that pair. Conversely, if we select 2K cakes and assign each selected cake to one of the three attributes with an even number of selected cakes per attribute, then pairing cakes assigned to the same attribute gives total price at least the assigned sum. Choosing the maximizing attribute for each resulting pair shows that the original maximum equals the maximum over such parity-feasible colored selections.

Thus the problem becomes: choose exactly 2K cakes, color each with one of three colors, require each color count to be even, and maximize the sum of the chosen color’s value. This is a selection problem with one cardinality constraint and only three parity bits. The unconstrained optimum is obtained by taking the 2K cakes with largest max-attribute value and coloring each by a maximizing attribute. Its parity mask has weight 0 or 2. If it is zero, it is already optimal. If not, the proposed exchange argument is promising: because selected cakes have max-value at least as large as unselected cakes, and recoloring away from a maximizing attribute never increases value, all local correction costs are nonnegative. Since parity lives in a two-dimensional XOR space, any feasible solution can be converted from the unconstrained optimum by recolor/swap operations whose XOR is the bad parity mask, and a subcollection of at most two operations has the same XOR and no larger total cost.

The main implementation risks are not the high-level reduction but the details: tie-breaking at the 2K boundary, choosing baseline colors only among maximizing attributes, handling empty unselected sets, using 64-bit arithmetic, and especially computing the minimum 0/1/2-operation correction without missing conflicts where two operations use the same selected or unselected cake. Storing only the single cheapest operation per parity mask is likely insufficient; a small conflict-aware candidate pool or direct top-two underlying candidates is needed.

### NEXT
- Implement the baseline parity-exchange method: sort by max-attribute value, take any 2K as the baseline, compute baseline total and odd-color mask, then compute the minimum loss using 0, 1, or 2 disjoint local operations. For each of the three nonzero parity masks, precompute candidate recolor operations from selected cakes and candidate swap operations from selected-to-unselected exchanges. For recolors, keep the two cheapest candidates for each source-target color pair. For swaps, keep candidates formed from the two smallest baseline-max selected cakes of the source color and the two largest unselected values of the target color. Evaluate one operation matching the bad mask and pairs of operations whose masks XOR to the bad mask, rejecting pairs that share a selected cake or an unselected cake. Pitfalls: arbitrary tie-breaking is safe only if selected max-values are never smaller than unselected max-values; baseline colors must be true maximizers; global cheapest-per-mask is not enough due to conflicts; handle cases where unselected set is empty or a color class has fewer than two candidates; totals require 64-bit integers.

- Try a Lagrangian relaxation / Aliens-trick approach: add a penalty λ for selecting a cake, and for fixed λ solve a tiny DP over the 8 parity masks where each cake independently chooses not-selected or one of the three colors with adjusted value. Track selected counts, especially min/max counts among optimal adjusted values, then binary search λ to force the count to cross 2K and recover the exact answer. Pitfalls: the value-as-a-function-of-count may not be concave once parity constraints are imposed; ties can produce multiple counts at the same λ; parity gaps may make exact 2K recovery nontrivial; proving correctness is harder than for the exchange method, and integer λ ranges with large values require careful handling.

- Try a fixed-quota assignment / min-cost flow formulation: enumerate or optimize over even numbers of cakes assigned to each attribute, say 2a, 2b, 2c with a+b+c=K, and for each quota triple solve a 3-color plus dummy assignment problem maximizing the sum. This can be viewed as a bipartite b-matching or transportation problem between cakes and color slots. Pitfalls: naive enumeration over quota triples is O(K^2) and too large; a full min-cost flow with up to 10^5 units is too slow; evenness is a modular constraint not directly handled by ordinary flow; taking top lists per attribute independently is invalid because the same cake cannot be assigned to multiple attributes; a practical version would need a strong convexity or greedy-exchange proof.

- Try attacking the original maximum-weight K-matching directly: edge weight is the maximum of three additive terms, so one might prune to a small candidate set of cakes or edges and run a matching or dynamic program on that candidate set. Pitfalls: general maximum-weight K-matching is far too expensive for N=10^5; there is no obvious constant-size candidate guarantee without essentially proving the parity-exchange property; a cake can be useful because of one attribute even if its max-attribute rank is not extreme; disjointness and parity can force using non-top cakes, so naive top-edge pruning is likely incorrect.

- Try an algebraic reformulation using the identity max(a,b,c) = (a+b+c+|a-b|+|b-c|+|c-a|)/2: rewrite the total as a vertex-selection term plus a matching term involving absolute values of sums of one-dimensional differences between attributes. Then explore whether the remaining pairing problem can be solved by sorting or geometric matching. Pitfalls: the pairing term is not additive over vertices and couples three absolute-value terms; optimal pairing for such convex pair-sum objectives is nontrivial and may still require general matching; differences can be negative, complicating simple sorting arguments; this route may not lead to a simple O(N log N) algorithm.
