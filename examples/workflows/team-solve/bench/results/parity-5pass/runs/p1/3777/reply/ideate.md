### NOTES
The core difficulty is that the subsequence constraint couples three dimensions at once: original order determines which chosen elements get plus or minus signs, the alternating sum must match an exact target, and the product must be maximized while staying under a small cap. The array is too long for exponential search, but the numeric ranges are favorable: values are at most 12, so the absolute alternating sum is bounded by roughly max_value * ceil(n/2) (at most 900 under the constraints), and the product cap is only 5000. Because all numbers are nonnegative, every valid product is either positive (no zero chosen) or exactly zero (at least one zero chosen). Positive products are always better than zero, so the problem can be split into finding the best positive product and separately deciding whether a zero-product subsequence can achieve the target sum. A major subtlety is that a prefix whose positive product already exceeds the limit can still lead to a valid zero product if a zero is chosen later, so over-limit positive states cannot be used for positive answers but their sums must still be remembered for zero feasibility.

Candidate approaches:

- Product-major bitset DP for positive products, plus a separate zero-feasibility DP. Maintain, for each parity and each product up to limit, a bitset of achievable alternating sums. Process positive numbers left to right, carrying old states for skipping, starting new one-element subsequences, and extending old even-parity states with a plus shift and old odd-parity states with a minus shift while multiplying the product. For zero products, maintain a small boolean DP over parity and whether a zero has been selected, using bitsets of sums only. Pitfalls: must snapshot states before processing each element to avoid reusing the same element; product 1 can leave the product unchanged while changing parity and sum; bitsets need an offset and masking; zero handling must include prefixes whose positive product exceeded the limit; the empty subsequence must not be counted as a valid positive answer.

- Sum-major product bitset DP. Maintain, for each parity and each achievable sum, a bitset of achievable positive products. Process positive numbers by shifting sums and transforming the product bitset by multiplication with the current value. Zero feasibility can still be handled separately. Pitfalls: multiplying a product bitset by a value is not a simple bit shift, so it requires iterating set bits or precomputed mapping tables; this can be much slower than shifting sum bitsets; memory is acceptable but transition cost is the main risk; product 1 and limit boundaries still need careful handling.

- Pareto or max-product pruning per sum and parity. Store only a subset of products for each parity and sum, attempting to discard products that cannot lead to a better final answer. Pitfalls: simple “keep only the maximum product” is incorrect because a larger product may overflow the limit after future multiplications while a smaller product could remain valid; exact dominance is subtle under a product cap; aggressive pruning can easily become unsound, and weak pruning degenerates into storing the full product set anyway.

- Meet-in-the-middle. Split the array into two halves, compute achievable parity/sum/product states for each half, then combine them. When concatenating a first-half subsequence and a second-half subsequence, the parity of the first half determines whether the second half’s alternating sum is added or subtracted, and the products multiply. Pitfalls: combining product pairs can become expensive even though the product cap is small; zeros require special handling because product zero can arise from either half; the empty subsequence must be excluded from the final answer; the sign-flip rule during concatenation is easy to get wrong; for n=150 this may not beat a full left-to-right DP.

- Full state bitset or factor-exponent representation. Encode all states consisting of parity, sum, and product into one or more large bitsets, or represent products by prime-exponent vectors since values are small. Each item transition would shift sum blocks and map product blocks. Pitfalls: the product transition is not a simple shift and may require expensive precomputed masks or tables; the full state space is large, around parity times sum-range times product-cap; zeros create an absorbing product-zero state that complicates the representation; this approach may be overkill and harder to optimize in practice.

### NEXT
- Implement the product-major positive-product DP with sum bitsets, together with a separate four-state zero-feasibility DP, as the primary solution candidate.
- Implement the sum-major product-bitset DP as an alternative if product-major transitions are too slow or for cross-validation.
- Implement an exact per-sum product-set DP with safe pruning, or full product bitsets per sum, to test whether any max-product-only simplification is possible.
- Implement a meet-in-the-middle state-combination approach for smaller cases or as a correctness oracle on random small inputs.
- Implement brute-force enumeration for very small arrays to validate edge cases involving zeros, ones, negative targets, and limit boundaries.
